# Popper and Falsifiability

## Source

- Karl R. Popper, *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1959).
- Stephen Thornton, "Karl Popper," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, substantive revision 2022.
- Working web source used during setup: <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/>

## Source Context

- Popper is addressing the demarcation problem: what makes a claim scientific rather than merely compatible with any observation.
- Thornton's SEP entry is useful here because it explains both the original logic of Popper's position and the later caveats and criticisms that matter in practice.
- The reference is relevant to agent prompts because it is less about laboratory science specifically and more about disciplined critical inquiry under uncertainty.

## Key Ideas

- Science starts from problems, not from passive observation. Theories are proposed as attempted solutions and then subjected to criticism.
- A strong theory makes risky predictions. The more a claim rules out, the more informative and testable it is.
- Confirmation alone is weak. A result counts more when it came from a test that could have shown the claim to be wrong.
- Corroboration is provisional. Surviving a test makes a theory usable for now, not final or certain.
- Observation is not theory-free. What counts as a test result depends partly on conventions, measurement, background assumptions, and shared judgment.
- Popper distinguishes the logic of falsifiability from the methodology of actual falsification. In real work, apparent counterexamples often require interpretation.

## Practical Caveats

- This source does not justify a naive rule that one failing test instantly settles every dispute. Popper's own view is more careful than that, and later critics pressed this point hard.
- The reference is best used as a discipline for forming and testing claims, not as a slogan for aggressive rejection.
- For engineering work, the strongest carryover is methodological: make your explanation vulnerable to evidence, and separate what is verified from what is inferred.
- The source supports local, piecemeal criticism better than sweeping claims of certainty.
